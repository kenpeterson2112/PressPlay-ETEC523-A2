Mayer's 12 principles

Mayer's principles describe how to combine words, sound and pictures so people learn from them. You will use them in every mission. They fall into three groups. Tap a group to see each principle and where it shows up on this page.

Reduce extraneous processing

Strip out anything that uses up attention without helping learning.

Coherence
Leave out extra material such as background music, side stories and decoration.On this page: the case study asks whether this holds for audio drama, which relies on music and sound effects.
Signalling
Use cues that point to what matters most.On this page: the study guide questions in Mission 3 tell listeners what to listen for.
Redundancy
Narration with visuals works better than narration, visuals and the same words as on-screen text.On this page: Mission 4 weighs up showing a full transcript during playback.
Spatial contiguity
Place words next to the things they describe.On this page: quiz feedback appears right under the question it answers.
Temporal contiguity
Present narration at the same time as the visuals it describes, not before or after.On this page: the video overview in Mission 2.

Manage essential processing

Help learners handle material that is complex by nature.

Segmenting
Break content into short chunks the learner paces.On this page: six short missions, and short listening segments in Mission 3.
Pre-training
Introduce key terms and concepts before the main lesson.On this page: this section, and the terms-first step in Mission 3.
Modality
Pair visuals with spoken words rather than printed text.On this page: the video overview in Mission 2 narrates its visuals.

Foster generative processing

Encourage learners to make sense of what they hear and see.

Multimedia
Words and pictures together work better than words alone.On this page: Mission 2 compares an audio-only podcast with a video overview of the same reading.
Personalisation
Use a conversational style, with “you” and “we”, rather than a formal one.On this page: the two-host podcast in Mission 2, and the script checklist in Mission 5.
Voice
A friendly human voice works better than a machine voice.On this page: Mission 2, where 47% of students found an AI voice robotic or monotone.
Image
Seeing the speaker on screen does not necessarily help learning.On this page: the video overview in Mission 2 shows the content, not a talking head.

Mission 1Foundations

Why audio?

Mayer's theory rests on three assumptions about how people learn from words and pictures. Tap each one and think about what it means for a listener with earbuds in.

People process sound and visuals through separate channels.

A podcast uses the auditory channel only. What could the visual channel add, and what could it overload?

Each channel can hold only so much at once.

A long episode with new vocabulary in every sentence can overload a listener. Pacing and structure matter.

Learners must actively organize and connect what they hear.

Passive listening is not enough. This is the case for questions before and after an episode.

Don't mix these up

Podcast: episodic audio, usually on demand.
Audiobook: a text read aloud, in full.
Lecture capture: a recorded class, often with slides.

Mission 3Questions that change listening

From passive listening to active retrieval

Audio on its own invites passive listening. Questions give learners a reason to listen. Two of Mayer's principles shape the design: Pre-training (key terms first) and Segmenting (short chunks the learner paces).

Pre-training: terms first

Define the key terms before the statistics. For the climate reading, that means rapid-onset and slow-onset hazards, and what "net migration" counts.

Listen in short segments

Short episodes with the learner in control of pace. Inline adaptive questions are a future goal, not part of this prototype.

Study guide questions, no answers

Open-ended prompts send learners back into the audio to search for answers. That is retrieval practice, not re-listening.

Evidence

Study guides made the difference

In a U.S. management information systems course (171 students), NotebookLM podcasts of textbook excerpts were paired with question-based study guides. Exam scores rose by about 3 points out of 15, and the gain was statistically significant. Final course grades did not change significantly. Students who engaged more with the podcasts did better on exams and in the course (Rezvani, 2026).

Case studyA real classroom

The Hollow in a Grade 6 class

The Hollow from GZM Shows is a modern retelling of Washington Irving's The Legend of Sleepy Hollow. Three sixth graders follow the trail of the Headless Horseman, and listeners decide whether he is a frightening ghoul or simply misunderstood. It is an example of a podcast series that teachers use to support literacy instruction and to build good listening skills. Mission 5Students as producers

Make a two-minute episode

Podcasts are also something students can create. Use Mayer's principles as a design checklist for the task.

The task

Students plan and record a two-minute episode explaining one idea, applying at least three principles.

  • Purpose: one idea, one audience.
  • Script: chunked, conversational, no filler.
  • Check: if AI drafts the script, fact-check the terminology against the source and edit.
  • Voice: their own, or a deliberate choice.
  • Audience check: a peer listens and answers two questions.
